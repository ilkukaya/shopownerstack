Many small service businesses set their hourly rate by asking what the shop down the road charges.
That number contains none of your costs, none of your unbillable time and none of your target
profit, which is one reason busy shops can still make little money. Here is the arithmetic that
produces a rate you can defend.

## What numbers do you need to set an hourly rate?

Three: billable hours per year, annual overhead, and the owner income plus profit the business has
to produce. Everything else is detail.

**Billable hours per year.** Not hours worked. Quoting, driving, invoicing, chasing payment and
buying parts are not billable. Field service software vendors such as FieldEdge publish technician
utilization targets in the range of roughly 60 to 85 percent of paid time for well-run teams. A
solo owner who also does the office work will usually sit below that. The honest way to get this
number is to track it for a month.

**Annual overhead.** Everything that is not labor on the job: vehicle, insurance, software, phone,
accountant, rent, tools, marketing. Pull a year of bank and card statements rather than working from
memory, because small recurring subscriptions are easy to forget.

**Owner income and target profit.** What you need to pay yourself, plus what the business should
keep. If profit is not in the calculation, it tends not to show up. For reference, the BLS reports a
median wage for employed plumbers, pipefitters and steamfitters of about $30.67 an hour in May 2025.
That is what an employee earns, not what a business needs to charge.

## What is the formula for a profitable hourly rate?

```
Hourly rate = (annual overhead + owner income + target profit) / billable hours
```

**Hypothetical example.** Say a solo plumber works 45 hours a week for 48 weeks (2,160 hours) and
bills about 60 percent of that, or roughly 1,300 hours. Their overhead is $46,000, they want $85,000
of income, and they want the business to keep $15,000:

```
(46,000 + 85,000 + 15,000) / 1,300 = $112.31 per hour
```

These inputs are illustrative, not a benchmark for plumbers. Use your own. The result is a floor,
before materials markup. Our [job pricing calculator](/tools/job-pricing-calculator/) runs the same
arithmetic with starting assumptions for each trade that you can overwrite.

## Why does the billable hours number matter most?

Because it moves the answer most. In the hypothetical above, raising billable time from 55 to 65
percent of hours worked lets the same business charge about 15 percent less for the same income, or
charge the same and keep the difference.

That makes your software a pricing question, not only an admin one. Every hour spent on paperwork has
to be paid for by the hours that are billed. Quoting from a phone on site instead of typing it up in
the evening changes the denominator.

## How should you mark up materials?

Price materials separately from labor, and consider a markup that varies by cost band. A tiered
matrix, with a higher percentage on cheap parts and a lower one on expensive parts, is common
practice in auto repair, because handling a $4 fitting costs roughly as much as handling a $400 part.

Some software builds this in. Tekmetric's support center documents configurable parts markup
matrices with cost ranges, which is one reason we cover it in our
[Tekmetric review](/reviews/tekmetric/). In the trades, check whether your price book supports cost
bands; many products apply a single markup unless you set it up otherwise.

## What is the most common pricing mistake?

Quoting a price that covers the job but not the unbilled work that produced it.

**Hypothetical example.** If you spend 90 minutes preparing a quote for a four-hour job, and you
win half the quotes you send, every job you win is carrying three hours of unpaid quoting. Either
speed up quoting or price for the fact that it is slow.

## How do you check whether your pricing is working?

Compare quoted margin to actual margin on completed jobs, every month. That needs actual time and
materials recorded against each job.

tiers. For the broader question of flat rate versus hourly, see our
