Most small service businesses set their hourly rate by asking what the shop down the road
charges. That number contains none of your costs, none of your unbillable time and none of
your target profit, which is why so many busy shops make no money.

Here is the arithmetic that produces a rate you can defend.

## What are the only three numbers you need?

**Billable hours per year.** Not hours worked. A solo operator working 45 hours a week for
48 weeks does 2,160 hours, but quoting, driving, invoicing, chasing payment and buying parts
are not billable. Realistically 55 to 65 percent of that is chargeable, so 1,200 to 1,400
hours.

**Annual overhead.** Everything that is not the labour on the job: vehicle, insurance,
software, phone, accountant, rent, tools, marketing. Add it up honestly - most people
under-count by a third because they forget the small recurring subscriptions.

**Target owner income and profit.** What you need to pay yourself, plus what the business
should retain. If you do not put profit in the calculation, you will not get any.

## What is the formula?

```
Hourly rate = (annual overhead + owner income + target profit) / billable hours
```

A solo plumber with $46,000 of overhead, wanting $85,000 of income and $15,000 of retained
profit, working 1,300 billable hours:

```
(46,000 + 85,000 + 15,000) / 1,300 = $112.31 per hour
```

That is the floor, before materials markup. Our
[job pricing calculator](/tools/job-pricing-calculator/) runs this with the benchmark
overhead and rate figures for each trade so you can sanity-check your own numbers.

## Why does the billable hours number matter more than the others?

Because it moves the answer most. Improve utilisation from 55 to 65 percent and the same
business can charge 15 percent less for the same income - or charge the same and take the
difference as profit.

That is why the software you use is a pricing question, not an admin question. Every hour
your team spends on paperwork is an hour that has to be paid for by the hours that are
billed. Quoting from a phone in two minutes instead of typing it up in the evening moves the
denominator.

## What about materials?

Price them separately and apply a markup by cost band. Cheap parts carry a higher percentage
markup than expensive ones - that is standard practice, not sharp practice, because the
handling cost of a $4 fitting and a $400 unit is roughly the same.

A shop applying a single flat markup to everything is usually losing several points of gross
profit on small parts without noticing. In auto repair,
[Tekmetric](/reviews/tekmetric/) makes this a configurable matrix; in the trades most
software leaves it to you.

## What is the most common mistake?

Quoting a price that covers costs but not the unbilled work that produced it. If it took you
90 minutes to quote a job and the job takes four hours, your effective rate is not what you
think. Either speed up the quoting or price for the fact it is slow.

## How do you check whether it is working?

Compare quoted margin to actual margin on completed jobs, monthly. If your software cannot
produce that comparison - and many cannot without the top tier - it is the single most
valuable report to go looking for. [Tradify](/reviews/tradify/) and
[Contractor Foreman](/reviews/contractor-foreman/) are the two products on this site that do
it best at a small-business price.
