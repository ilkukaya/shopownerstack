Flat rate is often sold to trades as an obvious upgrade from hourly billing. It is not automatic. It
tends to raise margin for shops that do repeatable work and measure it, and it can quietly erode
margin for shops that do neither.

## What is the difference between flat rate and hourly pricing?

With hourly (time and materials), the customer pays for the time the job takes, so the risk of a
slow job sits with the customer. With flat rate, the customer pays a fixed price for a defined job,
so the risk sits with you, and so do the gains when your team gets faster.

That last point is the core of the argument.

**Hypothetical example.** If a technician gets 20 percent faster at a repeated job, flat rate turns
that saved time into margin or capacity for another job. On hourly billing, the customer simply
pays 20 percent less.

## When does flat rate pricing work?

When three conditions hold together:

1. **The work repeats.** You do the same job types often enough to know how long they really take,
   including the bad days, not just the average.
2. **You measure actual time.** Real timer data against completed jobs, not "about two hours."
3. **You price for the spread, not the mean.** If a job usually takes 90 minutes but sometimes four
   hours, a price set at the average loses money whenever it goes wrong.

In our assessment, residential electrical, plumbing and HVAC service calls often meet these
conditions. Diagnostic work, restoration, remodels and work on older buildings often do not.

## What does your software need to support flat rate?

Two things: a price book of fixed-price line items, and time tracking against completed jobs so you
can compare what you charged with what it took.

Together they form the loop: quote from the book, measure the reality, adjust the book on a regular
schedule, for example quarterly. A shop that does the first without the second is guessing with
extra steps.

Some examples from vendor documentation. [Housecall Pro](/reviews/housecall-pro/) documents a
flat-rate pricing feature that calculates prices from your business inputs and applies material
markups by cost range, and it offers a pre-built price book powered by Profit Rhino as a paid
add-on. [Tradify](/reviews/tradify/) documents a job timer that records time against each job and
calculates cost and gross profit. Check which plan includes these features, and confirm you can
report on actual time versus quoted price before you commit to the model.

## What goes wrong most often with flat rate?

Prices get set once and never checked. A shop adopts a national price book, never measures its own
times, and finds out much later that some of its most common jobs have been sold below cost.

A national book is a starting point, not an answer. Your labor rate, drive times and technicians are
not the ones it was built from.

## Is there a middle option between flat rate and hourly?

Yes, and many established shops use it: flat rate for repeatable jobs, hourly for diagnostics and
anything unpredictable, with a diagnostic fee credited against the flat-rate repair if the customer
goes ahead.

That structure prices the risky work by time and the known work by outcome, which puts the risk
where each side can best manage it.

## What should you do before switching to flat rate?

Work out your true hourly cost first. Flat-rate prices built on a rate that does not cover your
overhead just lock the mistake in for longer. Use the
[job pricing calculator](/tools/job-pricing-calculator/) and read
[how to price a service job](/guides/how-to-price-a-service-job/) for the arithmetic, then pick your
ten most common jobs and time them for a month before you publish a single flat price.
