Flat rate is sold to trades as an obvious upgrade from hourly billing. It is not obvious. It
raises margin for shops that do repeatable work and measure it, and it quietly destroys
margin for shops that do neither.

## What is the actual difference?

Hourly: the customer pays for the time it takes. Risk sits with the customer, and your
efficiency gains go to them.

Flat rate: the customer pays a fixed price for a defined job. Risk sits with you, and your
efficiency gains stay with you.

That last clause is the entire argument. A technician who gets 20 percent faster at a
repeated job makes you 20 percent more margin on flat rate and nothing at all on hourly.

## When does flat rate work?

Three conditions, all of them:

1. **The work repeats.** You do the same job types often enough to know the real time
   distribution, not just the average.
2. **You measure actual time.** Not "about two hours" - real timer data against completed
   jobs.
3. **You price for the spread, not the mean.** If a job takes 90 minutes most of the time
   and four hours when it goes wrong, pricing at the average loses money on the tail.

Electrical and plumbing service work usually meet these. Diagnostic work, restoration and
anything on old buildings usually does not.

## What does your software need to support?

A price book of flat-rate line items with a price attached, and a timer that captures actual
time against completed jobs so you can compare.

Those two together are the mechanism: quote from the book, measure the reality, adjust the
book quarterly. A shop that does the first without the second is guessing with extra steps.

timer that survives the app being killed. Neither does both especially well, which is worth
knowing before you commit to the model.

## What goes wrong most often?

Shops set flat-rate prices from a national price book, never measure their own times, and
find out two years later that three of their most common jobs have been sold below cost the
entire time.

The national book is a starting point, not an answer. Your labour rate, your drive times and
your technicians are not the ones it was built from.

## Is there a middle option?

Yes, and most established shops use it: flat rate for the repeatable jobs, hourly for
diagnostics and anything unpredictable, with a diagnostic fee credited against the flat-rate
job if the customer proceeds.

That structure prices the risky work by time and the known work by outcome, which is the
right way round.
