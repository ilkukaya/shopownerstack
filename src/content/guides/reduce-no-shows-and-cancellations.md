A no-show in an appointment business is not just an empty hour. It is an hour you also staffed,
which makes it more expensive than a slot you never sold. Most salons and studios treat it as a
customer behavior problem. It is mostly a systems problem.

## What is a normal no-show rate?

Lower than the scary numbers you see in marketing, but it varies widely. Zenoti's 2025 benchmark
data puts the average salon on its platform at about a 3 percent no-show rate and an 8 percent
cancellation rate. Nail salons ran around a 16 percent cancellation rate with a very low no-show
rate, and medspas showed a higher no-show rate of about 5 percent.

Treat any published figure with care: it depends on who is measured and whether reminders and
deposits were already in place. Your own booking history is the real baseline. Pull last quarter's
no-shows and late cancellations before changing anything, so you can tell whether a change worked.

## Do reminder texts actually work?

Yes, and they have the best evidence behind them. A 2013 Cochrane review of seven studies with 5,841
participants found text reminders improved attendance at healthcare appointments compared with no
reminder. Healthcare appointments are not haircuts, but controlled trials in salons do not really
exist, so this is the best evidence available.

Wording matters too. Two randomized trials in a UK hospital trust, published in PLOS One in 2015,
found that a reminder stating the cost of a missed appointment cut the missed-appointment rate from
11.1 percent to about 8.4 percent, compared with the standard message. A reminder that names what
is at stake ("this 3-hour slot is reserved for you") is worth testing.

On timing, there is less hard data. In our assessment, two reminders cover most of it: one a day or
two out, early enough that the client can reschedule rather than skip, and one a few hours before.
More reminders beyond that mostly add noise.

## Does a card on file reduce no-shows?

It adds commitment at booking, which is the point. A client who has entered card details has made a
stronger promise than one who left a name and phone number, and it makes a no-show fee collectable
if you choose to charge one.

Most salon and spa booking systems support it. Turn it on for online booking first, since that is
where clients you do not know yet come from.

## When should you take a deposit?

On long or high-value services, set per service rather than across the board. A quick trim rarely
needs one; a four-hour color correction usually does, and clients booking one tend to expect it.

Software that applies the deposit automatically removes an awkward conversation. For example,
Boulevard's support center documents deposits charged as a percentage of the service at booking and
applied to the bill at checkout, plus a configurable cancellation policy. See our
[Boulevard review](/reviews/boulevard/) for the rest of the product. The underlying issue is that
many salons have a no-show policy and few enforce it, because enforcing it means someone at the
front desk confronting a regular. A policy the software enforces is one that actually happens.

## How do you refill a cancelled slot?

With a waitlist that clients can join themselves, worked quickly when a slot opens. A cancellation
you refill the same day costs you very little.

Check how automated the waitlist is before you rely on it. Some systems notify waitlisted clients
automatically when a slot opens; others simply record the request and leave staff to call down the
list. On a busy day, a manual list tends not to get worked, so ask the vendor to show you exactly
what happens when a slot frees up.

## What about class-based studios?

The bigger leak is usually failed recurring payments rather than no-shows: members whose card
expired, who never decided to leave, and whom nobody chased. Recurly's churn research counts this
as involuntary churn and finds it makes up a meaningful share of total subscription churn.

Judge studio software on how it handles a declined renewal. Momence's help center, for example,
describes configurable automatic retries on a failed membership renewal, an immediate notice to the
member asking them to update their card, and the membership going inactive after the final failed
retry. Compare that with what your current system does, and see our reviews of
[Momence](/reviews/momence/) and [Mindbody](/reviews/mindbody/).

## What does not work?

Charging fees you never collect, policies that live only on your website, and asking staff to have
the difficult conversation. All three feel like action and change little.

The practical order: measure your current rate, turn on two reminders with specific wording, put a
card on file at online booking, add deposits on your longest services, and make sure someone (or
something) works the waitlist. For more on how booking products compare, see
[Boulevard vs Mindbody](/compare/boulevard-vs-mindbody/).
